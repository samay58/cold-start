Proposed cross-cutting gate logic
Everything in this subsection is a proposal, not approved doctrine.
Proposed test for the company’s actual bet
A candidate bet should be evaluated through five questions:
Coverage: Does it explain the company’s most consequential product, customer, economic, and organizational choices?
Commitment: Has the company made costly, persistent, or constraining commitments consistent with it?
Consequence: Would failure of this bet materially damage the company’s intended path to success?
Continuity: Is it reflected in the company’s recent direction, not merely a past phase or one announcement?
Dramatic-fact challenge: If the most vivid event were removed from the evidence pack, would this still look like the company’s central bet?
A bet should not be central merely because it has the most impressive customer, largest financing, most dramatic failure, or most quotable announcement.
Proposed positive threshold for a current strategy
A current strategy clears only when the audit establishes:
Mechanism existence: Evidence shows the relevant mechanism is actually operating.
Strategic effect: The mechanism materially affects buyer choice, economics, competitive conduct, or the company’s operating model.
Present relevance: The mechanism is doing strategic work now.
Sibling resolution: The nearest plausible sibling labels have been considered and the distinction is evidenced.
Independence: The label is not merely a second name for a mechanism already captured elsewhere.
Explanatory value: Removing the label would make the account of the company’s bet materially worse.
A strategy can be factually applicable and still fail the current-strategy threshold.
Proposed minimum content of a sibling distinction
A sufficient sibling reason should contain:
The evidence or mechanism that creates the ambiguity.
The deciding question from the rubric.
The evidence-backed answer to that question.
Why the rejected sibling would misdescribe the mechanism.
When both siblings are selected, the distinct causal role played by each.
“X is a better fit than Y” is not a sufficient sibling reason.
Proposed treatment of weak evidence
Not yet: There is an observed precursor, a credible causal path, a named missing condition, and a defined observation that would promote it to current.
Open question: A specific obtainable fact could materially change the mapping, but the available record cannot answer it.
Insufficient evidence: The record cannot support or refute the mechanism. This is a disposition, not a conclusion about the company.
Omit from reader output: The question is generic, low-impact, or unsupported by any concrete lead.
Unsupported speculation: There is no observed precursor or the causal bridge depends on an invented premise.
Proposed stop rule
The stored judgment should say “nothing stands out” only when no strategy clears the evidence, materiality, independence, and explanatory-value gates for any material company bet.
One supported current strategy is not “nothing.”
2. All-80 mapping rubric
Rubric conventions
Positive evidence identifies evidence capable of supporting a candidate. It does not automatically make the strategy current.
Disqualifying evidence means affirmative evidence against the mechanism. Missing evidence is insufficient evidence, not disqualification.
Nearest sibling strategies are the most likely confusions, not an exhaustive list.
Every sibling question must be answered from evidence IDs in the company audit.
Every selected label must use the exact canonical name.
Accumulation
Usership
Positive evidence: Evidence that participation by additional active users makes the product more useful to existing users, such as more counterparties, better matching, richer shared content, broader interoperability, or more valuable user-generated contributions.
Common false positives: A large customer count; famous customer names; social proof; revenue growth; usage data that improves the vendor’s model but does not increase user-to-user utility; scale that improves uptime.
Nearest sibling strategies: Aggregation, Emergence, Omnipresence, Antifragility, Reliability.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Does another user’s participation directly increase the value received by existing users, or does scale merely make the company larger, more available, more trusted, or operationally stronger?
Disqualifying evidence: Users operate in isolated instances; utility does not change as participation grows; additional users degrade the experience without offsetting benefits; value comes entirely from the vendor’s own inputs.
Completeness
Positive evidence: Buyers can perform the entire defined job in one product or system and retire, avoid, or materially reduce dependence on other tools.
Common false positives: Many features; an “end-to-end” marketing claim; a broad general-purpose product; a marketplace containing many offerings; several products sold together.
Nearest sibling strategies: Versatility, Bundling, Aggregation, Composability, Omnipresence.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Does the offering eliminate the buyer’s need for another tool across the defined job, rather than merely serving many tasks, combining modules, or arriving in a package?
Disqualifying evidence: Core workflows still require external tools; customers routinely assemble a best-of-breed stack; coverage is broad but shallow; the company deliberately remains a point solution.
Aggregation
Positive evidence: The company creates an environment or marketplace that admits broad participation from suppliers, creators, developers, buyers, or other parties, and value comes from gathering them in one venue.
Common false positives: A large user base; a company-owned catalog; a tightly curated marketplace; an API ecosystem without meaningful participation; a list of integrations.
Nearest sibling strategies: Usership, Curation, Union, Emergence, Omnipresence.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is the mechanism broad participation inside a shared environment, rather than selective choice, common rules, crowd-generated intelligence, or user-to-user utility?
Disqualifying evidence: The company owns or produces nearly all supply; participation is tightly closed by design; there is no shared environment; outside participation does not contribute meaningful value.
Diversification
Positive evidence: Revenue, customers, suppliers, geographies, products, or capital sources are meaningfully independent, such that weakness in one stream does not seriously impair the whole.
Common false positives: A long customer list concentrated in one market; several products bought from the same budget; nominal geographic diversity with shared risks; “diversity” in an organizational sense.
Nearest sibling strategies: Decentralization, Versatility, Aggregation, Modularity.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is the advantage reduced dependence on any one economic or operating stream, rather than distributed competence, product breadth, or broad participation?
Disqualifying evidence: A customer, supplier, market, product, or financing source remains materially concentrated; nominally separate streams share the same failure mode; one stream economically sustains the rest.
Omnipresence
Positive evidence: The product is available, embedded, integrated, distributed, or accepted across nearly every relevant place or moment where the need arises, often with default placement or habitual acceptance.
Common false positives: A large addressable market; operation in many countries; high brand awareness; aggregation; bundling; many integrations that leave important contexts uncovered.
Nearest sibling strategies: Aggregation, Bundling, Standardization, Usership, Completeness.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is the advantage that the product is already present or accepted wherever the relevant need occurs?
Disqualifying evidence: Important channels or contexts remain absent; customers must actively search for or install it; competitors occupy major default positions; availability is broad but not consequential.
Cloning
Positive evidence: Growth occurs by reproducing a proven whole operating unit, such as a store, franchise, production cell, service pod, deployment, or facility, with minimal redesign and repeatable economics.
Common false positives: Copying a competitor; autoscaling software; modular components; standardized procedures; geographic expansion that requires substantial local customization.
Nearest sibling strategies: Predictability, Standardization, Modularity, Blitzing, Copycat.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Does expansion come from replicating the same complete unit, rather than reusing components, following a common standard, or copying someone else’s method?
Disqualifying evidence: Each expansion is bespoke; local conditions fundamentally change the unit; performance depends on a nonreplicable founder or artisan; replicated units have materially different economics.
Price
Affordability
Positive evidence: The buyer’s total cost is sustainably lower than relevant alternatives, and that difference materially affects adoption, retention, market access, or replacement decisions.
Common false positives: A free trial; temporary subsidies; low sticker price with high integration or operating cost; internal efficiency that is not passed to buyers; being cheaper than a luxury option.
Nearest sibling strategies: Efficiency, Bundling, Skimming, Simplicity.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is the buyer choosing the product because the total cost is lower, rather than because it is bundled, internally efficient, or structurally simple?
Disqualifying evidence: The company prices at parity or a premium and buyers are indifferent to price; total cost is higher; low pricing is temporary and unsupported by the operating model.
Luxury
Positive evidence: The company deliberately charges more, invests in labor-intensive quality or service, restricts access, or uses price and exclusivity as part of the product’s status and desirability.
Common false positives: High prices caused by low scale or inefficiency; third-party recognition; superior quality without deliberate premium positioning; an expensive enterprise contract.
Nearest sibling strategies: Prestige, Craftsmanship, Scarcity, Rarity, Charm.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is deliberate expensiveness or exclusivity itself part of the value, rather than merely the result of cost, recognition, limited supply, or quality?
Disqualifying evidence: The company competes primarily on discounts or value; premium pricing is considered a problem; customers would prefer the same offering to be cheaper without loss of meaning or status.
Skimming
Positive evidence: The company captures a small fee, take rate, transaction slice, or resource increment across a very large volume of activity.
Common false positives: Low prices; ordinary subscriptions; an advertising business generally; data extraction without a tiny-per-flow economic mechanism; marketplace participation without a skimming model.
Nearest sibling strategies: Affordability, Aggregation, Parasitism, Chokepoint.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Does the company win by taking a very small amount from each of many flows?
Disqualifying evidence: Economics require large margins on a small number of transactions; volume is low; the company is paid in large fixed contracts unrelated to transaction flow.
Bundling
Positive evidence: The product is included free or at negligible incremental cost with something customers already want, and the host product materially changes its distribution, adoption, or economics.
Common false positives: A multi-product suite; a volume discount; an integrated workflow; a product that happens to be cross-sold; Completeness.
Nearest sibling strategies: Completeness, Affordability, Omnipresence, Union.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Would this product have materially less adoption or bargaining power if it were not packaged with the independently desired product?
Disqualifying evidence: Customers buy it primarily as a standalone product; the package is optional or immaterial; the host offering has no independent demand.
Time
Heritage
Positive evidence: A legacy spanning generations materially affects buyer trust, identity, status, provenance, or preference in the present.
Common false positives: An old incorporation date; long founder tenure; nostalgia-themed marketing; simple endurance; a historical archive unknown to buyers.
Nearest sibling strategies: Endurance, Authenticity, Prestige, Organic.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Does inherited, multigenerational legacy itself create present value?
Disqualifying evidence: There is no meaningful generational continuity; the brand has been reset or disconnected from its past; buyers do not know or care about the legacy.
Craftsmanship
Positive evidence: The product receives more human time, care, precision, judgment, or specialized attention per unit than relevant alternatives, and that difference is visible in the result.
Common false positives: Premium branding; automated accuracy; a slow process; handmade claims without comparative evidence; high cost alone.
Nearest sibling strategies: Precision, Luxury, Organic, Specialization.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is extra time and attentive making the source of advantage, rather than technical accuracy, natural maturation, narrow expertise, or premium pricing?
Disqualifying evidence: Production is designed to minimize time and attention; quality is generated through ordinary automation; there is no evidence of additional care or skilled judgment.
Organic
Positive evidence: Value depends on a biological, ecological, cultural, social, or relationship process whose natural clock cannot be materially accelerated without damaging the result.
Common false positives: Bootstrapped growth; word-of-mouth acquisition; slow company growth; “organic” marketing language; craftsmanship.
Nearest sibling strategies: Heritage, Rarity, Craftsmanship, Endurance.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Does value depend on a natural process whose timing is irreducible?
Disqualifying evidence: Capital, automation, or process compression can produce the same outcome; time is merely delay rather than a source of quality or defensibility.
Endurance
Positive evidence: The company or system has operated continuously through changing conditions, and persistence itself creates trust, knowledge, installed position, access, or confidence.
Common false positives: An old founding date; a durable physical product; reliable uptime over a short period; heritage symbolism.
Nearest sibling strategies: Heritage, Reliability, Durability, Predictability.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is uninterrupted survival or operation over time the operative advantage?
Disqualifying evidence: Material shutdowns or discontinuities break the claimed continuity; the current entity or product is not meaningfully connected to the historical operation; persistence does not affect present outcomes.
Uniqueness
Specialization
Positive evidence: Resources, talent, product design, data, or process are concentrated on a narrow customer, problem, domain, or workflow, and that focus produces superior fit or performance.
Common false positives: A small market; one product; niche branding; high accuracy; selective curation.
Nearest sibling strategies: Versatility, Precision, Craftsmanship, Curation.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is narrow scope itself what enables superior competence?
Disqualifying evidence: The company pursues broad general competence; performance is not better in the niche; the narrow position is accidental or temporary.
Versatility
Positive evidence: The same core product, capability, or team performs competently across materially different tasks, settings, or customer needs.
Common false positives: Many separate products; extensive customization; coverage of every step in one workflow; two distinct strengths described as a Hybrid.
Nearest sibling strategies: Completeness, Hybrid, Malleability, Composability.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is one competence broadly useful across many tasks, rather than eliminating all tools for one job, changing form for each user, or combining two distinct competencies?
Disqualifying evidence: Performance deteriorates outside a narrow core; breadth depends on unrelated products or teams; versatility exists only in a roadmap.
Hybrid
Positive evidence: Two distinct competencies or qualities are both materially strong in the same offering or company, especially when they commonly trade off or are rarely found together.
Common false positives: Any two strategy labels; broad generalism; “best of both worlds” marketing; a partner supplying one of the two capabilities; two separate products.
Nearest sibling strategies: Versatility, Metamorphosis, Specialization, Composability.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Are two independently demonstrated competencies present simultaneously, rather than one broad competence, one customizable form, or separate optimized states?
Disqualifying evidence: One side is weak, aspirational, or entirely outsourced; the two capabilities are not genuinely distinct; the combination is ordinary in the relevant category.
Divergence
Positive evidence: The company makes a deliberate, substantive departure from category conventions in product, business model, design, distribution, or behavior, and the difference affects strategic outcomes.
Common false positives: Cosmetic branding; simply being new; irreverent language; inconsistent execution; following a smaller trend.
Nearest sibling strategies: Irreverence, Unpredictability, Charm, Copycat.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is the advantage a substantive creative difference from the norm, rather than anti-establishment posture, surprise, affection, or imitation?
Disqualifying evidence: The product follows the dominant design or business model; differences disappear in actual use; the departure has no material effect.
Authenticity
Positive evidence: Provenance, chain of custody, authorship, identity, or source can be verified in a market where counterfeits, replicas, impersonation, or false origin are meaningful problems.
Common false positives: A founder story; transparency; heritage; respected branding; “authentic voice.”
Nearest sibling strategies: Heritage, Transparency, Prestige, Security.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is value created by proving that the item, content, person, or entity genuinely comes from the claimed source?
Disqualifying evidence: Counterfeiting or false provenance is not relevant; the source cannot be verified; buyers do not care about origin; the company relies on imitation itself.
Rarity
Positive evidence: Supply is inherently constrained by nature, location, biology, time, a unique asset, or a nonrepeatable event.
Common false positives: Deliberate production limits; temporary shortages; high price; low manufacturing capacity; exclusive distribution.
Nearest sibling strategies: Scarcity, Organic, Authenticity, Luxury.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Would quantity remain limited even if the producer wanted to expand it?
Disqualifying evidence: Supply can be increased at will; the limitation comes from policy, capital, or current production capacity rather than an inherent constraint.
Scarcity
Positive evidence: The company intentionally restricts quantity, access, membership, release timing, or availability, and the restriction contributes to demand, status, pricing, or control.
Common false positives: Stockouts; naturally rare inputs; small-company capacity; premium pricing; selection through Curation.
Nearest sibling strategies: Rarity, Luxury, Highest bidder, Curation.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is supply or access intentionally restricted by the company rather than naturally constrained or merely expensive?
Disqualifying evidence: The company attempts to satisfy all demand; shortages are accidental; limits are not enforced; availability expands whenever demand appears.
Secrecy
Positive evidence: Operative knowledge is protected, tacit, hidden, or so entangled that competitors cannot reproduce the mechanism without unavailable know-how or reconstructing the whole system.
Common false positives: Closed-source software; security controls; privacy protections; patents that publicly disclose the method; complexity by itself.
Nearest sibling strategies: Security, Privacy, Obscurity, Espionage.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is copying hard because the essential knowledge is inaccessible or cannot be disentangled?
Disqualifying evidence: The method is public or readily reverse-engineered; talent and customers can transfer it easily; the claimed secret is not necessary to performance.
Irreverence
Positive evidence: The company repeatedly rejects category traditions, taboos, authorities, or expected behavior, and that counter-cultural stance attracts users or enables strategic choices.
Common false positives: Edgy copy; humor; one controversial campaign; generic Divergence; frequent surprises.
Nearest sibling strategies: Divergence, Charm, Unpredictability, Last-mover.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is the company gaining specifically from disregarding convention or authority?
Disqualifying evidence: Operational choices remain conventional; the posture disappears under commercial pressure; target buyers primarily value established legitimacy.
Offense
Violence
Positive evidence: The company deliberately uses superior force or resources to eliminate, consume, or render competitors incapable, rather than merely outperforming them.
Common false positives: Strong growth; aggressive sales; routine price competition; ordinary acquisitions; forceful rhetoric.
Nearest sibling strategies: Sabotage, Scavenging, Blitzing, Highest bidder.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is the operative mechanism direct destruction or consumption of opposing capacity?
Disqualifying evidence: Competitors remain healthy and capable; wins come from ordinary product preference; there is no targeted effort to eliminate opposing capacity.
Litigation
Positive evidence: Lawsuits, intellectual-property enforcement, regulatory complaints, or other legal processes impose material cost, delay, or restriction on opponents.
Common false positives: Routine contract disputes; ordinary compliance; defensive legal work; possessing patents without using them offensively.
Nearest sibling strategies: Deterrence, Monopoly, Sabotage, Security.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is legal burden itself weakening opponents, rather than merely threatening retaliation, defending assets, or enforcing an authorized monopoly?
Disqualifying evidence: Legal action is incidental or purely defensive; opponents face no meaningful burden; the company does not use legal process strategically.
Nettlesomeness
Positive evidence: The company’s tactics cause competitors to overreact, misprice, misallocate resources, abandon good positions, or otherwise make avoidable mistakes.
Common false positives: Being annoying; moving quickly; unpredictable conduct without evidence of opponent error; directly damaging a competitor.
Nearest sibling strategies: Unpredictability, Lure, Sabotage, Irreverence.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is the advantage created by the opponent’s mistaken response?
Disqualifying evidence: No opponent mistake is observed or reasonably inferred; the company creates the damage directly; the alleged error would have occurred anyway.
Sabotage
Positive evidence: The company deliberately weakens a competitor’s critical structure, complement, distribution channel, supplier base, technology, or defense.
Common false positives: Ordinary competition; hiring talent; price reductions; litigation generally; provoking an opponent into a mistake.
Nearest sibling strategies: Violence, Nettlesomeness, Parasitism, Infiltration.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Does the company directly degrade an opponent’s system or support structure?
Disqualifying evidence: Harm is only the incidental result of winning customers; no critical structure is targeted; the opponent’s underlying system remains intact.
Parasitism
Positive evidence: The company depends on a host’s audience, infrastructure, data, content, distribution, or economics while extracting disproportionate value and leaving the host to bear the main cost.
Common false positives: A normal platform partnership; a consensual transaction fee; interoperability; a mutually beneficial dependency; using third-party infrastructure at market rates.
Nearest sibling strategies: Symbiosis, Skimming, Infiltration, Puppeteering, Scavenging.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is the mechanism one-way extraction from a continuing host, rather than mutual dependence, a tiny consensual fee, covert entry, or control over behavior?
Disqualifying evidence: The host receives comparable benefit and can price the relationship; the company supplies its own critical resource; extraction is absent or immaterial.
Scavenging
Positive evidence: The company systematically acquires customers, talent, intellectual property, assets, locations, or distribution from failing, exhausted, or distressed competitors at advantaged terms.
Common false positives: Ordinary acquisitions; poaching from healthy rivals; Last-mover timing; restructuring a business the company already owns.
Nearest sibling strategies: Last-mover, Violence, Parasitism, Highest bidder.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Does the advantage depend on the target already being weakened or dying?
Disqualifying evidence: Targets are healthy; the company itself caused the weakness; assets are purchased through ordinary competitive bidding without distress advantage.
Espionage
Positive evidence: Credible evidence shows the company obtains nonpublic competitor information and uses it strategically.
Common false positives: Public benchmarking; lawful reverse engineering of a visible product; normal competitive intelligence; customer discovery; protecting the company’s own secrets.
Nearest sibling strategies: Secrecy, Infiltration, Copycat, Mimicry.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is genuinely secret information being acquired from the opponent?
Disqualifying evidence: Inputs are public, consensually shared, or independently discovered; no nonpublic competitor knowledge is used.
Swarming
Positive evidence: Many individually weak or limited agents coordinate a concentrated offensive action that overwhelms a stronger target.
Common false positives: A large user base; a distributed workforce; crowd-generated intelligence; many employees executing one centralized campaign.
Nearest sibling strategies: Herding, Emergence, Blitzing, Decentralization.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Are many individually weak units jointly attacking, rather than gathering for defense, producing collective knowledge, or executing a centrally resourced campaign?
Disqualifying evidence: The agents are individually powerful; their activity is uncoordinated; the purpose is defensive, productive, or merely participatory.
Highest bidder
Positive evidence: The company deliberately pays more than alternatives to secure exclusive talent, rights, supply, distribution, locations, or access.
Common false positives: High compensation; premium customer pricing; ordinary procurement; expensive acquisitions; broad resource expenditure.
Nearest sibling strategies: Blitzing, Scarcity, Monopoly, Violence.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is exclusivity won specifically by outpaying competitors?
Disqualifying evidence: The resource remains available to others; pricing is at market; nonprice factors determine access; no exclusivity results.
Chokepoint
Positive evidence: The company controls a gateway, interface, distribution point, standard implementation, resource, or passage that competitors or customers must traverse.
Common false positives: Popularity; high market share; Omnipresence; broad integration; an official monopoly right; a useful but optional platform.
Nearest sibling strategies: Monopoly, Omnipresence, Standardization, Union.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Must relevant participants pass through this controlled point to achieve the outcome?
Disqualifying evidence: Practical bypasses are common and economic; the passage is optional; control belongs to another actor; customers can substitute around it without material penalty.
Puppeteering
Positive evidence: The company gains embedded control over another actor’s behavior through governance rights, incentives, software, contractual authority, or a dependency that lets it direct decisions.
Common false positives: Influence; recommendations; platform rules that participants can ignore; Alliance; Parasitism; ordinary management control over employees.
Nearest sibling strategies: Parasitism, Infiltration, Centralization, Symbiosis.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Does the company direct the host’s behavior, rather than merely extracting from, entering, coordinating, or depending on it?
Disqualifying evidence: The host retains meaningful autonomy; incentives are optional; the relationship can be ignored or replaced without consequential loss of control.
Defense
Deterrence
Positive evidence: A visible and credible ability and willingness to retaliate makes attack, entry, appropriation, or adverse conduct less attractive.
Common false positives: Security controls; a large balance sheet; lawsuits already filed; vague threats; strong brand recognition.
Nearest sibling strategies: Security, Litigation, Violence, Neutrality.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is protection created by expected punishment after an attack, rather than by blocking access, imposing legal burden now, or remaining non-belligerent?
Disqualifying evidence: Retaliation is not credible or communicated; attacks occur without consequence; the company lacks the ability or willingness to impose cost.
Reliability
Positive evidence: The product requires less maintenance, fails less often, experiences less downtime, or recovers with less burden than relevant alternatives, and that difference affects buying or use.
Common false positives: Many users; long company history; predictable output; security certification; a generic service-level agreement; customer names.
Nearest sibling strategies: Predictability, Endurance, Durability, Usership, Precision.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Does the mechanism reduce failure and upkeep, rather than create user-to-user utility, low variance, accuracy, physical strength, or longevity?
Disqualifying evidence: Frequent failures or high maintenance burden; performance is merely at category parity and immaterial; growth in usage makes operation less reliable.
Predictability
Positive evidence: Outputs, delivery times, costs, quality, or processes repeat with little deviation, allowing buyers or operators to plan around them.
Common false positives: Uptime; high accuracy; a standard data format; routine schedules; a repeatable internal process with no buyer value.
Nearest sibling strategies: Reliability, Precision, Standardization, Endurance.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is low variance valuable even when no failure has occurred?
Disqualifying evidence: Outcomes vary materially; bespoke judgment dominates; customers cannot forecast delivery or output; surprise is part of the intended product.
Unpredictability
Positive evidence: Deliberate surprise, variability, or changing tactics make the company difficult for opponents to anticipate and protect or strengthen its position.
Common false positives: Frequent pivots; organizational chaos; Agility; creative product design; inconsistent execution.
Nearest sibling strategies: Agility, Nettlesomeness, Divergence, Irreverence.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is unpredictability itself strategically useful against another actor, rather than a side effect of adaptation, creativity, or disorder?
Disqualifying evidence: The company follows a stable and transparent playbook; surprise primarily harms customers or internal execution; no opponent-level benefit exists.
Decentralization
Positive evidence: Distributed nodes, teams, suppliers, regions, or competencies provide redundancy and allow continued operation when one component fails.
Common false positives: Remote work; community participation; distributed ownership; modular software; many offices; delegated tasks without redundant competence.
Nearest sibling strategies: Distributed ownership, Modularity, Reliability, Antifragility, Centralization.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Does distribution remove a material single point of failure?
Disqualifying evidence: A critical resource or decision still depends on one node; distributed parts share the same failure mode; no genuine redundancy exists.
Security
Positive evidence: Architecture, controls, operating history, audits, or buyer behavior show resistance to theft, confiscation, compromise, or unauthorized access.
Common false positives: A privacy policy; protection of trade secrets; compliance badges alone; high reliability; obscurity.
Nearest sibling strategies: Privacy, Secrecy, Deterrence, Reliability.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is the protected harm unauthorized taking or access, rather than unwanted disclosure, copying, or expected retaliation?
Disqualifying evidence: Known control weaknesses contradict the claim; repeated preventable compromise remains unresolved; the system depends on uncontrolled access.
Privacy
Positive evidence: Data minimization, local processing, encryption, restricted disclosure, verified policies, or contractual practices protect people or customers from unwanted revelation of information.
Common false positives: General Security; secrecy of company intellectual property; anonymous branding; compliance language unsupported by practice.
Nearest sibling strategies: Security, Secrecy, Transparency, Obscurity.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is the central harm unwanted disclosure of personal or customer information?
Disqualifying evidence: The business model depends on broad disclosure or sale; operating practices contradict the promise; users lack meaningful control or protection.
Durability
Positive evidence: Physical design, materials, testing, field performance, repairability, or service life show resistance to wear, impact, damage, or degradation.
Common false positives: Software uptime; company longevity; Reliability; Craftsmanship; a long warranty without physical evidence.
Nearest sibling strategies: Reliability, Endurance, Craftsmanship, Organic.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is the object physically resistant to damage or degradation?
Disqualifying evidence: There is no physical artifact; the product is intentionally disposable or fragile; ordinary use causes premature failure.
Neutrality
Positive evidence: Repeated conduct, governance, incentives, and economic structure demonstrate long-term nonalignment or nonaggression, making the company a trusted safe intermediary.
Common false positives: Generic fairness claims; broad partnerships; regulatory compliance; an open platform; serving customers on several sides of a dispute.
Nearest sibling strategies: Union, Transparency, Alliance, Decentralization.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Do participants trust the company because it credibly will not take sides, retaliate, or compete against them?
Disqualifying evidence: Self-preferencing; partisan control; selective enforcement; direct competition with dependent participants; repeated retaliation against ecosystem members.
Obscurity
Positive evidence: Remaining little known or difficult to detect materially reduces competition, regulation, attack, imitation, or predation, and the low profile is preserved.
Common false positives: Small market share; poor marketing; Secrecy; Camouflage; private ownership.
Nearest sibling strategies: Secrecy, Camouflage, Privacy, Infiltration.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is protection created by not being noticed at all?
Disqualifying evidence: Visibility is essential to acquisition, status, or distribution; the company publicizes the same activity; relevant adversaries already know and target it.
Antifragility
Positive evidence: Shocks, attacks, failures, volatility, or increased use systematically improve future capability, data, resilience, economics, or position.
Common false positives: Merely surviving stress; redundancy; performing postmortems; learning from customers; a growing user base; ordinary Iteration.
Nearest sibling strategies: Reliability, Decentralization, Iteration, Usership, Agility.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Does exposure to stress leave the system stronger than before, rather than merely intact or ordinarily improved through a managed feedback process?
Disqualifying evidence: Stress only consumes buffers or causes damage; improvements do not accumulate; the system returns to baseline at best.
Deception
Camouflage
Positive evidence: The product, actor, or behavior deliberately resembles its surrounding environment so gatekeepers or adversaries fail to detect or distinguish it.
Common false positives: White-labeling for convenience; native user experience; a low-profile company; copying a specific competitor; fitting category conventions.
Nearest sibling strategies: Mimicry, Obscurity, Infiltration, Decoy.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is concealment achieved by blending into the background, rather than impersonating a specific actor, staying unknown, or crossing a defense?
Disqualifying evidence: Distinct visibility is intended; no detection threat exists; resemblance does not conceal the product or actor.
Mimicry
Positive evidence: The company superficially adopts the traits of a specific trusted, dangerous, legitimate, or familiar actor so others mistake its identity or treat it differently.
Common false positives: Product compatibility; Copycat business models; Camouflage; using established category patterns; visual resemblance without deception.
Nearest sibling strategies: Camouflage, Copycat, Decoy, Infiltration.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is the purpose to be mistaken for a particular other entity or type?
Disqualifying evidence: Origin is clearly disclosed; similarity is purely functional; no mistaken identity or altered treatment occurs.
Decoy
Positive evidence: A visible product, offer, signal, roadmap, target, or activity intentionally draws attention away from the company’s real asset, vulnerability, or move.
Common false positives: A secondary product; a loss leader; a public roadmap that later changes; Lure; ordinary experimentation.
Nearest sibling strategies: Lure, Camouflage, Nettlesomeness, Mimicry.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is the target being diverted away from what actually matters, rather than attracted into a disadvantageous position?
Disqualifying evidence: The visible object is genuinely central; no concealed asset or move exists; the shift in attention is accidental.
Lure
Positive evidence: An attractive offer deliberately induces a target to enter a position where the company can capture, constrain, exploit, or disadvantage it.
Common false positives: Good marketing; a free product; Low-friction; Bundling; ordinary customer acquisition; a mutually beneficial entry offer.
Nearest sibling strategies: Decoy, Parasitism, Bundling, Low-friction.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Does the attraction function as a trap with a strategically adverse consequence for the target?
Disqualifying evidence: The exchange remains transparent and mutually beneficial; no concealed capture or dependency follows; users can leave without consequential disadvantage.
Infiltration
Positive evidence: The company enters a defended organization, market, workflow, or system below notice or around formal gatekeepers, then establishes position.
Common false positives: Bottom-up adoption that is knowingly permitted; easy onboarding; ordinary integration; native design; Camouflage without crossing a defense.
Nearest sibling strategies: Camouflage, Parasitism, Puppeteering, Low-friction.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Did the company cross a meaningful defense without the relevant gatekeeper noticing or authorizing the entry?
Disqualifying evidence: Entry is explicit, approved, and visible; no meaningful gate or defense exists; concealment is absent.
Timing
First-mover
Positive evidence: The company entered the relevant arena before credible rivals, and acting first created a durable asset such as users, data, rights, standards, locations, learning, or habit.
Common false positives: The earliest founding date; inventing a category term; the first press announcement; current market leadership; historical novelty without retained advantage.
Nearest sibling strategies: Second-mover, Last-mover, Heritage, Usership.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is the current advantage causally traceable to acting first?
Disqualifying evidence: The early lead dissipated; later entrants accumulated equivalent assets; “first” is defined narrowly after the fact; timing created no durable advantage.
Second-mover
Positive evidence: The company moved quickly after a pioneer, copied validated elements, avoided the pioneer’s mistakes, and benefited from following soon after proof.
Common false positives: Any Copycat; entering an established market; ordinary Iteration; entering after the market fully matured.
Nearest sibling strategies: Copycat, Last-mover, Iteration, First-mover.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is the advantage specifically the speed and learning of following immediately after the first mover?
Disqualifying evidence: Entry came much later; the method was independently developed; no learning from the pioneer is evident; the company entered before the approach was validated.
Last-mover
Positive evidence: The company deliberately waited while others spent resources on failed technical, regulatory, product, or business-model approaches, then committed after uncertainty had cleared.
Common false positives: Any late entrant; slow execution; Scavenging distressed assets; a fast follower copying the first validated winner.
Nearest sibling strategies: Second-mover, Scavenging, Curation, First-mover.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Did waiting through others’ failed approaches create the advantage?
Disqualifying evidence: Lateness was accidental; no avoided cost or learning is evident; the market was already locked up; the company merely copied the winner after the fact.
Accreditation
Monopoly
Positive evidence: A license, concession, charter, patent right, spectrum allocation, or other governing-body approval grants exclusive control over a resource or market.
Common false positives: Dominant market share; a Chokepoint; a de facto standard; a strong intellectual-property portfolio without exclusive authorized control.
Nearest sibling strategies: Chokepoint, Litigation, Standardization, Highest bidder.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Does the exclusivity derive from a governing body’s authorization?
Disqualifying evidence: Equivalent licenses are broadly available; no formal grant exists; control is purely commercial; the right has expired or no longer confers exclusivity.
Prestige
Positive evidence: Authoritative awards, degrees, certifications, institutional endorsements, or recognized accreditation materially change buyer selection, willingness to pay, or trust.
Common false positives: Top-tier investors; a prominent cap table; famous customers; celebrity associations; media attention; founder pedigree; awards unknown to buyers.
Nearest sibling strategies: Luxury, Heritage, Authenticity, Curation.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Would the buyer’s decision materially change without the authoritative recognition?
Disqualifying evidence: Recognition is unknown or irrelevant to the buyer; it only affects investors or recruiting; buyers choose for price or performance regardless; the supposed authority lacks credibility.
Curation
Positive evidence: The company deliberately excludes, ranks, selects, or groups options using demonstrated judgment, and buyers rely on that selection to reduce search or raise expected quality.
Common false positives: Aggregation; a recommendation algorithm with no meaningful selectivity; narrow Specialization; Prestige; a small catalog caused by limited supply.
Nearest sibling strategies: Aggregation, Specialization, Prestige, Centralization.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is value created by what the company deliberately leaves out and how it groups what remains?
Disqualifying evidence: The system accepts nearly everything; selection is determined only by payment or raw popularity; buyers do not trust or rely on the choices.
Collaboration
Union
Positive evidence: Potential competitors adopt common rules, governance, or protocols that reduce friction among them while they remain separate participants.
Common false positives: A bilateral partnership; a de facto technical standard; a trade association without operative rules; unilateral platform terms.
Nearest sibling strategies: Alliance, Standardization, Neutrality, Herding.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Are potential competitors coordinating through a common rule system?
Disqualifying evidence: One company unilaterally dictates the arrangement; there are no shared rules or enforcement; the participants are not potential competitors.
Alliance
Positive evidence: Independent organizations coordinate technology, distribution, resources, legitimacy, or market access for material mutual benefit while remaining strategically separable.
Common false positives: A routine vendor contract; a customer logo; an integration listing; a partnership announcement; Symbiosis; Union.
Nearest sibling strategies: Symbiosis, Union, Highest bidder, Bundling.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Can each party remain strategically viable and replace the relationship without breaking its core model?
Disqualifying evidence: The relationship is trivial or transactional; benefit is one-way; mutual dependence is central to each party’s operation; the parties no longer remain meaningfully independent.
Emergence
Positive evidence: Decentralized interactions among participants produce useful knowledge, order, quality, prediction, or behavior that no participant or central actor supplied alone.
Common false positives: A large user base; Aggregation; crowdsourced labor following a central answer key; popularity voting; user-generated content that does not produce collective intelligence.
Nearest sibling strategies: Usership, Aggregation, Swarming, Centralization, Curation.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Does the collective interaction generate an outcome that neither the participants individually nor a central planner provided?
Disqualifying evidence: Output is centrally determined; the crowd merely supplies volume; the collective result is no better or different than the individual inputs.
Centralization
Positive evidence: Concentrated decision rights produce coherence, speed, consistency, bargaining power, control, or accountability.
Common false positives: A founder-led company; a centralized database; Curation; ordinary management hierarchy; a Monopoly.
Nearest sibling strategies: Decentralization, Union, Curation, Puppeteering.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is the strategic advantage caused by one decision-making authority controlling the relevant system?
Disqualifying evidence: Key actors retain independent vetoes; authority is fragmented; the central bottleneck creates cost without offsetting advantage.
Standardization
Positive evidence: Independent actors converge on a common format, protocol, interface, or practice, reducing integration, switching, coordination, or transaction friction.
Common false positives: An internal operating procedure; a predictable process; formal Union rules; a proprietary format used only inside one vendor’s customer base.
Nearest sibling strategies: Union, Predictability, Modularity, Omnipresence, Chokepoint.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Has alignment become a shared norm across independent actors rather than one organization’s internal or imposed rule?
Disqualifying evidence: Incompatible variants remain dominant; adoption is confined to one company; the common format does not materially reduce friction.
Symbiosis
Positive evidence: Two independent parties co-specialize such that each gains material benefit and becomes meaningfully dependent on the other’s continued function.
Common false positives: A partnership; an integration; a customer-vendor relationship; Alliance; Parasitism; Bundling.
Nearest sibling strategies: Alliance, Parasitism, Bundling, Union.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Would either party’s core value or economics materially weaken if the other disappeared, and is that dependence mutual?
Disqualifying evidence: Either party can replace the relationship easily; value flows primarily one way; benefit is incidental; the dependency exists only because of a temporary contract term.
Herding
Positive evidence: Many smaller actors cluster, pool resources, or coordinate chiefly to protect themselves from a larger threat or competitor.
Common false positives: Aggregation; Union; a user network; a Swarming attack; attendance at an industry event; ordinary coalition-building.
Nearest sibling strategies: Swarming, Union, Aggregation, Decentralization.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is the group’s primary strategic function collective defense against a stronger actor?
Disqualifying evidence: The grouping exists for commerce, convenience, discovery, offense, or social participation; no shared larger threat is present.
Distributed ownership
Positive evidence: Users, workers, contributors, or community members possess meaningful economic or governance ownership rights in the system.
Common false positives: A distributed user base; open-source contributors; a token without enforceable rights; advisory voting; operational Decentralization.
Nearest sibling strategies: Decentralization, Union, Transparency, Centralization.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Do participants actually own part of the system, its residual value, or meaningful governance control?
Disqualifying evidence: Rights are symbolic, revocable, or non-economic; ownership remains concentrated; participants have voice or access but no ownership.
Transparency
Positive evidence: Product workings, code, pricing, governance, sourcing, decision processes, or performance are inspectable in a way that materially creates trust or accountability.
Common false positives: Marketing disclosure; an open-source repository that does not expose the operative process; Authenticity; a privacy policy; publishing selective metrics.
Nearest sibling strategies: Authenticity, Privacy, Neutrality, Secrecy, Curation.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Do participants trust the company because they can inspect how it works or decides?
Disqualifying evidence: Important workings remain hidden or misleading; disclosure is selectively curated to create a false impression; trust does not depend on visibility.
Speed and scale
Iteration
Positive evidence: An evidenced loop connects user, market, model, or operational feedback to rapid changes, and subsequent evidence shows that the changes alter outcomes or feed the next cycle.
Common false positives: A release count; frequent announcements; fundraising; many experiments; fast growth; a large engineering team.
Nearest sibling strategies: Agility, Antifragility, Second-mover, Precision.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: What feedback entered the loop, what changed because of it, and what evidence shows that the changed system produced the next round of learning?
Disqualifying evidence: Changes are unrelated to feedback; releases are cosmetic; cadence is not quick relative to the category; no loop from observation to change to subsequent outcome exists.
Efficiency
Positive evidence: Comparable capability is delivered with less compute, labor, capital, energy, time, inventory, or another material resource, and the advantage persists under relevant conditions.
Common false positives: A lower buyer price; fewer features; outsourcing costs; a small team; Simplicity; lower resource use caused by lower quality.
Nearest sibling strategies: Affordability, Simplicity, Precision, Skimming.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Does the company use fewer total resources to produce the same relevant capability?
Disqualifying evidence: Resource savings come with lower performance; costs are shifted to customers or partners; no comparable baseline exists; the advantage disappears at scale.
Agility
Positive evidence: The company or product changes direction, configuration, resource allocation, or operating response easily when the external environment changes.
Common false positives: Frequent pivots; ordinary Iteration; unpredictable behavior; user customization; emergency reaction.
Nearest sibling strategies: Iteration, Malleability, Unpredictability, Modularity.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Can the system adapt to an external change with low delay and low restructuring cost?
Disqualifying evidence: Architecture, contracts, incentives, or organization are rigid; adaptation requires a rebuild; frequent changes reflect chaos rather than easy adaptation.
Precision
Positive evidence: Accuracy, tolerance, targeting, calibration, or exactness materially exceeds relevant alternatives and improves the outcome.
Common false positives: Reliability; Predictability; Craftsmanship; Specialization; polished output; high confidence language.
Nearest sibling strategies: Reliability, Predictability, Craftsmanship, Specialization.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is the advantage the correctness or exactness of each output?
Disqualifying evidence: Error rates or tolerances are comparable or worse; the task does not reward exactness; claimed accuracy is unmeasured or defined against an irrelevant baseline.
Blitzing
Positive evidence: The company compresses a large deployment of capital, people, inventory, subsidies, or attention into a short, concentrated campaign to seize position.
Common false positives: Rapid growth; high annual spending; Highest bidder; ordinary expansion through Cloning; a large launch.
Nearest sibling strategies: Highest bidder, Swarming, Cloning, Violence.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is the advantage created by the sudden temporal concentration of resources?
Disqualifying evidence: Expenditure is gradual or routine; no concentrated objective exists; intensity follows success rather than creating a strategic position.
Composability
Positive evidence: Primitives or components can be assembled and recombined into materially different higher-order configurations, including configurations not predefined by the vendor.
Common false positives: A modular architecture; integrations; customization; broad Versatility; a plugin directory.
Nearest sibling strategies: Modularity, Malleability, Versatility, Completeness.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Can users or systems recombine the parts into materially different wholes?
Disqualifying evidence: Assembly is fixed; every new configuration requires bespoke vendor work; components only function in one prescribed arrangement.
Modularity
Positive evidence: Units have clear boundaries and can be replaced, upgraded, deployed, removed, or allowed to fail independently while the larger system continues.
Common false positives: Composability; a “microservices” label; Cloning; Decentralization; simply having several components.
Nearest sibling strategies: Composability, Decentralization, Cloning, Standardization.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is independence of units the advantage, even when users do not create novel combinations?
Disqualifying evidence: Components are tightly coupled; changes cascade through the system; units cannot operate or change independently; interfaces are unstable.
Ease
Intuitiveness
Positive evidence: Users correctly understand concepts, controls, or next actions on first encounter or with minimal instruction, reducing training and interpretation burden.
Common false positives: Few steps; simple internal architecture; attractive design; familiarity produced through Mimicry; expert users finding the product easy.
Nearest sibling strategies: Simplicity, Low-friction, Charm, Mimicry.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is the product easy because users immediately grasp how it works?
Disqualifying evidence: Users require substantial training, documentation, or expert help; the mental model is routinely misunderstood; ease arrives only after a difficult setup.
Fun
Positive evidence: Enjoyment, play, amusement, surprise, or intrinsic pleasure materially drives adoption, engagement, retention, or advocacy.
Common false positives: Charm; superficial gamification; an entertainment-adjacent category; a visually attractive interface; a Lure.
Nearest sibling strategies: Charm, Lure, Intuitiveness, Divergence.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Do users keep choosing the product because the activity itself is enjoyable?
Disqualifying evidence: Use is purely instrumental; engagement persists despite a disliked experience; amusement is decorative and does not alter behavior.
Simplicity
Positive evidence: The product, process, or architecture has few components, dependencies, modes, or failure points, and that minimal structure creates robustness, focus, or ease of operation.
Common false positives: An intuitive interface over a complex system; Low-friction onboarding; a small feature set; Efficiency; hiding complexity from the user.
Nearest sibling strategies: Intuitiveness, Low-friction, Efficiency, Reliability.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is the underlying design genuinely minimal, rather than merely easy for the user to experience?
Disqualifying evidence: Complexity is only concealed; many coupled dependencies remain; minimalism removes necessary capability without creating a strategic advantage.
Low-friction
Positive evidence: The company removes meaningful steps, approvals, switching effort, setup, payment barriers, integration work, waiting, or other resistance between intent and completion.
Common false positives: Intuitiveness; a low price; Bundling; Infiltration; Simplicity; a polished interface.
Nearest sibling strategies: Intuitiveness, Simplicity, Infiltration, Affordability.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is the advantage the removal of resistance or hassle between intent and completion?
Disqualifying evidence: Procurement, setup, switching, or use remains burdensome; friction is merely shifted to another actor; reduced steps do not change behavior.
Charm
Positive evidence: Distinctive personality, aesthetics, voice, or interaction creates affection, warmth, forgiveness, loyalty, or advocacy.
Common false positives: Prestige; Luxury styling; Fun; polished branding; Divergence; general customer satisfaction.
Nearest sibling strategies: Fun, Prestige, Luxury, Divergence.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Does warmth or personality itself make users more loyal or willing to choose the product?
Disqualifying evidence: Aesthetic appreciation does not affect choice or retention; personality is generic or inconsistent; buyers value status or function rather than affection.
Transformation
Malleability
Positive evidence: The same product, object, or system can be readily reshaped, configured, edited, or customized for a user or context without rebuilding its core.
Common false positives: Many use cases; Composability; organizational Agility; separate product modes; bespoke professional services.
Nearest sibling strategies: Versatility, Composability, Agility, Metamorphosis.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Can the user or context alter the form itself with low effort?
Disqualifying evidence: The form is fixed; customization requires a vendor-led rebuild or substantial bespoke work; changes are merely cosmetic.
Metamorphosis
Positive evidence: The system transforms between distinct states, each optimized for a different function, and the transition itself is integral to value.
Common false positives: Customization; simultaneous Hybrid competence; broad Versatility; ordinary version upgrades; cosmetic modes.
Nearest sibling strategies: Malleability, Hybrid, Versatility, Agility.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Does the system switch between discrete optimized forms rather than remain one flexible form or hold both strengths at once?
Disqualifying evidence: Only one operative state exists; modes differ only cosmetically; transitions are so costly that users effectively use separate products.
Copycat
Positive evidence: The company deliberately replicates another actor’s method, format, style, or operating approach and gains from adopting the proven pattern.
Common false positives: Replicating its own units through Cloning; Mimicry intended to deceive; Second-mover timing; following a required Standardization.
Nearest sibling strategies: Second-mover, Mimicry, Cloning, Divergence.
The deciding question that separates it from those siblings: Is the mechanism copying someone else’s approach, independent of whether the copy was rapid, deceptive, or repeatedly cloned?
Disqualifying evidence: The approach was independently developed; similarity is required by a shared standard; copied elements are immaterial to the company’s advantage.
3. Evidence taxonomy
These are categorical definitions. They should govern the structured audit, even though the eventual prose should not carry certainty badges.

Source-level discipline
The claim type does not repair a weak source.
For example:
Source says: “The company claims 99.99% uptime.”
Valid observed fact: “The company reports 99.99% uptime.”
Invalid observed fact: “The product has 99.99% uptime.”
Possible reasonable inference: “Reliability may influence enterprise adoption,” but only with additional evidence connecting uptime to buying behavior or category differentiation.
Possible judgment: “Reliability is current,” but only after the full strategy gates are met.

5. Tightened unusual-pair standard
This entire standard is a proposal, except for the approved line: “A pair has to exclude something before it counts as unusual.”
A pair must first consist of two independently current strategies
Before evaluating the pair:
Both component strategies must independently clear the current-strategy gates.
Neither may depend on the pair to become valid.
The same evidence cannot be double-counted unless it supports two genuinely different causal roles.
A current strategy and a not-yet strategy may be recorded as a possible future interaction, but not as the selected current unusual pair.
What the pair must exclude
The excluded item must be a concrete, plausible strategic alternative available to relevant competitors. It may be:
a product architecture
a business model
a distribution model
a pricing model
a governance model
an operating model
a customer promise
a resource-allocation priority
an optimization target
The excluded item cannot be:
“competitors without these strategies”
“poor execution”
“being undifferentiated”
“not having the company’s advantages”
an implausible straw-man choice
a merely descriptive opposite with no strategic consequence
A sufficient exclusion claim contains:
The common alternative: What relevant peers normally choose or optimize.
The incompatibility: Why pursuing both selected strategies makes that alternative costly, constrained, or impossible.
The accepted cost: Evidence that the company actually bears or accepts the associated constraint.
The strategic consequence: Why giving up that alternative matters.
Example structure:
> Relevant competitors normally optimize for [alternative]. Combining [Strategy A] and [Strategy B] requires the company to constrain or abandon that alternative because [causal reason]. The company has accepted this through [evidenced commitment or cost].

What makes the interaction unusual
Co-occurrence is not interaction.
The audit must show at least one directional causal relationship:
A enables B.
A amplifies B.
A protects B.
A converts B from a generic capability into a strategic advantage.
A makes the economics of B viable.
B makes A more credible, durable, or difficult to imitate.
The interaction must matter to the company’s actual bet. Two labels that coexist in different corners of the business do not form a pair unless the evidence connects them.
What makes the combination harder to copy
The copying-difficulty claim must identify linked changes a competitor would need to make. Valid mechanisms include:
replacing a core architecture
changing a business model
cannibalizing an existing revenue stream
giving up a customer segment
developing a path-dependent asset
changing governance or ownership
rebuilding distribution
acquiring a scarce complement
reorganizing incentives, talent, or operations
accepting an economic tradeoff the incumbent is structured to avoid
Invalid copying-difficulty claims include:
“execution is hard”
“the company has a head start”
“competitors would need both strategies”
“culture is difficult to copy” without evidence
“the product is technically complex” without identifying why the joint mechanism requires that complexity
Evidence rule for the reference class
A reference class requires positive evidence for substitutability.
The audit should establish:
The same buyer or decision-maker.
The same important job or outcome.
Competition for the same budget, workflow position, transaction, attention, or resource.
Broadly comparable operating or economic constraints.
Named peers or substitutes, not only an industry label.
Evidence IDs showing why the named peers belong.
“Enterprise software,” “AI companies,” or “consumer marketplaces” will usually be too broad.
The narrowest defensible class should be used. It may be widened only when the company is creating a new buying decision or when direct competitors are too few to represent the strategic alternatives.
Evidence rule for the normal category choice
The normal choice must be established through affirmative evidence, such as:
representative peer products, pricing, architecture, or distribution
customer comparison evidence
procurement patterns
category standards or accepted operating conventions
credible industry research
repeated strategic behavior across named substitutes
The following are not sufficient:
failure to find an example of the pair
one competitor behaving differently
the model’s general knowledge of the category
a broad claim that “most companies usually…”
absence of contrary evidence
The audit must preserve:
the named normal choice
the peers or category evidence supporting it
contrary examples
why the evidence is representative enough for the claim
When thin peer evidence forces an open question
The pair should be recorded as an open question, not selected, when:
the peer set is too small or unstable to establish a normal choice
category boundaries are disputed and no buyer-level reference class resolves them
evidence covers only one conspicuous competitor
sources describe products but not the relevant strategic choice
the claimed rarity depends primarily on absence
the internal interaction is plausible, but category unusualness cannot be established
the structural tradeoff is asserted but not evidenced in the company or peers
A strong internal interaction can still be recorded. It is not enough to establish an unusual pair without evidence of the relevant alternative and category context.
When no pair should be selected
Select no pair when:
either component strategy is not independently current
no concrete alternative is excluded
the reference class is unsupported
the normal category choice is inferred from silence
the two strategies merely coexist
the interaction is generic or additive
copying difficulty reduces to “doing two hard things”
the mechanisms operate in unrelated company bets
the pair is common in the relevant category
a sibling-label error creates the apparent pair
evidence is too thin to distinguish a pair from a plausible story

7. Decision brief for the five unresolved choices
Decision 1: When one company should be split into multiple strategic bets
Real tradeoff
A single company-level bet encourages coherence and prevents analysts from turning every product into its own thesis. It can also flatten genuinely different businesses that win through different buyers, economics, competitors, and mechanisms.
A permissive split rule captures complexity but creates an easy escape from judgment. Any contradictory evidence can be assigned to a new sub-bet.
Strongest option
Split only when each proposed bet has:
an independently evidenced win mechanism
consequential company commitments tied to that mechanism
a material structural difference in customer decision, economics, competitive set, operating model, or product architecture
a demonstrated reason that one combined thesis would distort the strategy mapping
Products, geographies, or customer tiers do not by themselves establish separate bets.
Strongest objection
This rule may keep related but strategically different businesses combined when they share a buyer or infrastructure. It also introduces a judgment about what counts as “material distortion,” which can itself be manipulated.
Proposal: exact sentence to approve
> Split a company into multiple strategic bets only when each proposed bet has an independently evidenced win mechanism and consequential commitments, and combining them would conceal a material difference in customer decision, economics, competitive set, operating model, or product architecture. Product count, geography, or organizational structure alone is not enough.

Decision 2: Which selection dimensions must be explicit fields
Real tradeoff
Separate fields expose hidden reasoning and prevent a generic, well-evidenced label from outranking the company’s actual mechanism. Requiring every dimension for all 80 strategies can also produce mechanical filler, false precision, and a large audit burden.
Strongest option
Make all five dimensions explicit for any strategy that has positive support or is advanced as current, not yet, or open:
evidence strength
centrality
materiality
distinctiveness
independence
Allow “not reached” after the evidence gate for strategies with no positive support.
Keep explanatory value as a separate required selection gate. Do not combine the dimensions into a score.
Under this approach:
Evidence, materiality, independence, and explanatory value determine current selection.
Centrality orders strategies relative to the company’s bet.
Distinctiveness helps identify generic category requirements and informs presentation.
No dimension can compensate numerically for failure of another gate.
Strongest objection
The dimensions overlap. Centrality and explanatory value can become two versions of the same judgment; distinctiveness and independence can also blur. Models may fill every field with repetitive prose without improving decisions.
Proposal: exact sentence to approve
> Record evidence strength, centrality, materiality, distinctiveness, and independence as separate categorical judgments for every strategy with positive support; strategies that fail the evidence gate may mark the remaining fields “not reached.” Do not combine the dimensions into a score. Evidence, materiality, independence, and explanatory value are current-selection gates, while centrality and distinctiveness govern ordering and interpretation.

Decision 3: Evidence needed for a pair’s reference class and normal category choice
Real tradeoff
A strict evidence rule sharply reduces invented pairs and vague claims about what “most competitors” do. It may also prevent pair selection in young or poorly documented categories where the interaction is genuinely unusual but peer evidence is thin.
Strongest option
Require:
a buyer-level definition of the reference class
named credible substitutes
evidence that they compete for the same decision, budget, workflow position, or outcome
positive evidence of the normal alternative across representative peers or credible category sources
contrary examples and an explanation of why they do not overturn the normal-choice claim
When the normal choice cannot be positively established, the pair remains an open question.
Strongest objection
This may systematically under-select pairs in emerging categories, private markets, and technical infrastructure markets where peer strategy is difficult to observe. Structural tradeoffs may be clearer than public peer evidence.
Proposal: exact sentence to approve
> An unusual pair requires a sourced reference class of credible substitutes for the same buyer decision and positive evidence that a concrete alternative is the normal category choice. Absence of observed peers using the pair is not evidence. When the reference class or normal choice cannot be established from representative peer or category evidence, record the pair as an open question.

Decision 4: What proves a historical mechanism is still doing strategic work
Real tradeoff
Requiring recent repetition prevents stale labels. Some strategies, however, are cumulative assets. A First-mover position, Heritage, Endurance, installed standard, or historical distribution advantage may remain current even when the original actions are no longer being repeated.
Strongest option
A historical strategy counts as current when either:
recent evidence shows the mechanism is still operating, or
a present material outcome remains causally dependent on the accumulated historical asset.
The second route requires an explicit bridge. “It happened in the past” is not enough.
Examples of legitimate continuing outcomes might include:
current buyers still choosing because of the legacy
current network value depending on the accumulated user base
current distribution depending on historical default placement
current economics depending on an installed standard
current access depending on a still-valid authorized right
Strongest objection
Current causal dependence can be difficult to prove from public evidence. The rule may produce false negatives when a moat remains real but is so embedded that companies and customers no longer discuss it explicitly.
Proposal: exact sentence to approve
> A historical mechanism counts as current only when recent evidence shows that it still operates, or when a present material outcome is demonstrably dependent on the accumulated historical asset. Historical existence alone is insufficient.

Decision 5: Whether 12 to 24 months is a hard not-yet boundary, a default, or unnecessary
Real tradeoff
A hard boundary constrains speculation and keeps the output relevant to the company’s near-term reality. It can be arbitrary across industries with different product, regulatory, biological, infrastructure, or adoption clocks.
No boundary avoids false precision but allows remote possibilities to occupy the not-yet set.
A default boundary creates discipline while permitting justified exceptions, but those exceptions can become a loophole.
Strongest option
Use 12 to 24 months as the default, not a hard boundary.
An exception should require:
a sector-specific reason for a different maturation clock
an observable precursor now
a staged causal path
a named next condition
a reason the strategy belongs in the active analytical horizon rather than long-range speculation
The bias remains toward present evidence. A remote endpoint should not qualify merely because it is plausible.
Strongest objection
Models may invoke “sector-specific timing” whenever they want to preserve a weak future story. A hard 24-month limit would be easier to validate and enforce.
Proposal: exact sentence to approve
> Treat 12 to 24 months as the default horizon for a not-yet strategy, not a hard boundary. Any exception must be justified by a sector-specific maturation clock, an observable precursor, a staged causal path, and a named next condition, with a continuing bias toward evidence already visible now.
