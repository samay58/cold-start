1. Analyze the company at the level where the real bets exist
The system should begin at the company level but should not assume the company has one unified strategic bet.
It may identify materially different bets by product, business line, customer group, or market when those parts differ in meaningful ways, such as:
 the customer and purchasing decision
 the problem being solved
 the economic model
 the competitive set
 the mechanism through which the company expects to win
 the resources and organizational commitments required
The system should not split a company merely because it has several products. Conversely, it should not manufacture one elegant company-wide thesis when the evidence shows separate businesses pursuing different strategies.
The deciding question should be:
> Would combining these activities into one strategic bet conceal an important difference in how the company expects to win?

If yes, analyze them separately. The final output can then explain how the bets relate at the company level, including whether one funds, strengthens, constrains, or conflicts with another.
2. Do not impose a target number of strategies
Every one of Steph’s 80 strategies should be evaluated against the evidence. There should be no rule saying a company must have two, three, or four strategies.
The result may be:
 no supported strategies
 one unusually clear strategy
 several reinforcing strategies
 different strategy sets for materially different company bets
The safeguard against label sprawl should be an explanatory-value test, not a numerical cap.
A strategy should appear in the selected set only when all of the following are true:
 There is sufficient evidence that its mechanism exists.
 The mechanism materially affects how the company competes, operates, or is chosen.
 It contributes something distinct from the other selected strategies.
 Removing the strategy would make the explanation meaningfully less accurate.
That fourth test is important. A strategy can be technically applicable without deserving inclusion.
For example, almost every competent infrastructure company values reliability. Reliability should only be selected when it is a material buying criterion, a meaningful operating commitment, or an actual source of advantage. “The product needs to work” is not enough.
Do not collapse everything into one confidence score
“Confidence” is directionally right, but one scalar score would hide several different judgments.
A strategy can be:
 strongly evidenced but peripheral to the company’s bet
 central to the company’s bet but supported partly through inference
 distinctive but not yet economically important
 material but common throughout the category
The structured output should therefore preserve separate dimensions:
Evidence strength: How directly and comprehensively is the mechanism supported?
Strategic centrality: How much does it explain the company’s major choices and expected success?
Materiality: Does it meaningfully affect customer choice, economics, competitive behavior, or operating performance?
Distinctiveness: Does it distinguish the company from relevant alternatives?
Independence: Does it add a mechanism not already captured by another selected strategy?
The selection process should work in this order:
 Eliminate strategies that do not meet the evidence threshold.
 Eliminate strategies that are immaterial or duplicative.
 Rank the surviving strategies primarily by strategic centrality.
 Use evidence strength, materiality, and distinctiveness to explain and refine the ordering.
This prevents a generic, easily proven label from outranking the mechanism that actually explains the company.
3. The reference class for an unusual pair
The correct reference class is:
> The narrowest defensible set of companies and strategic alternatives competing for the same buyer decision under broadly similar economic and operating constraints.

In practice, this begins with companies that share:
 the same customer or buyer
 the same important job to be done
 the same budget or purchasing decision
 a reasonably similar business and delivery model
 enough competitive overlap that buyers could credibly choose one instead of another
This should include direct competitors and close substitutes. It should not automatically include every company in the broad industry.
For example, “enterprise software,” “consumer,” or “artificial intelligence” will usually be too broad to determine whether a pair is unusual. The relevant class may instead be something like developer tools purchased by individual engineers and later standardized by an enterprise, or security systems bought by a centralized chief information security officer.
When to broaden the reference class
Broaden it one level when:
 the category is genuinely new
 there are too few meaningful direct competitors
 the company is creating a new buying decision
 conventional category boundaries obscure the real substitutes
The broader group should still compete for the same underlying customer outcome or budget. The method should never broaden the reference class merely to make a pair look rare.
Observed rarity is not sufficient
A pair is not unusual merely because few current competitors happen to use it.
The system should also ask:
> Would a competent, well-funded entrant naturally choose both strategies?

If the answer is yes, the pair is probably not strategically unusual. Competitors may simply be early, slow, or poorly executed.
A pair becomes meaningfully unusual when combining the strategies requires a difficult choice, contradiction, sacrifice, or operating commitment. It should exclude a common alternative.
The pair record must therefore name:
The reference class
The normal category choice
What the pair excludes or makes difficult
Why a competitor would struggle to reproduce both simultaneously
How the two mechanisms reinforce each other
A valid unusual-pair claim should have the following structure:
> Most relevant competitors choose or optimize for X. This company combines A and B, which requires it to give up, constrain, or solve X differently. A strengthens B through a specific mechanism. Copying the pair would require a competitor to change multiple linked parts of its product, economics, distribution, organization, or reputation.

If the analysis cannot fill in each part, it should not select a pair.
Thin categories require restraint
A small or new category creates uncertainty, not proof of unusualness.
When the peer group is too thin to establish what is normal, the system may still select a pair if there is clear evidence of a structural tension between the strategies and a concrete reason they are difficult to combine. Otherwise, the pair should remain an open question.
4. Use company history to understand the present, with a strong bias toward now
The system should use the company’s full relevant history when that history helps explain:
 why the company made its current choices
 how a mechanism developed
 whether an advantage is cumulative
 whether the company has consistently maintained a strategic commitment
 whether an apparent strategy is persistent or episodic
But a historical fact does not establish a current strategy by itself.
A strategy should count as current only when the mechanism:
 has operated in the company’s real history
 remains active or consequential now
 affects present customer behavior, company economics, competitive conduct, or operating decisions
Older evidence can establish origin and causality. Recent evidence must establish continued relevance.
The method should explicitly reject claims such as:
 “The company historically had a developer community, therefore Community is a current strategy.”
 “The founders once pursued partnerships, therefore Alliance remains strategically important.”
 “The company was once prestigious, therefore Prestige still affects purchasing decisions.”
The question is not whether the strategy ever existed. It is whether its mechanism is still doing strategic work.
5. Define “not yet” within a one-to-two-year horizon
A “not yet” strategy should represent a credible mechanism that could become strategically meaningful within approximately the next 12 to 24 months.
It must have all of the following:
An observable precursor today
The company has already built, launched, invested in, contracted for, or organizationally committed to something relevant.
A clear causal path
The existing precursor could produce the strategy through an identifiable mechanism.
A named missing condition
The analysis can state exactly what has not yet happened.
A plausible 12-to-24-month path
The missing condition could reasonably be met in that period without requiring an entirely different company.
A testable future observation
The system can state what evidence would promote the strategy from “not yet” to current.
For example:
> The company has begun collecting proprietary workflow data, but there is not yet evidence that the accumulated data improves product outcomes or customer retention. This would become a current strategy if additional usage demonstrably produces better results that competitors cannot obtain without comparable data.

Unsupported speculation lacks one or more of those elements.
Statements such as these should not qualify:
 “The company could eventually build a network effect.”
 “Its investor base may create prestige.”
 “Its partnerships might become symbiotic.”
 “Its data could become proprietary.”
 “Its large market may generate scale.”
Those are possibilities, not evidenced strategic trajectories.
Revised shape of the decision procedure
The procedure should now produce:
 The company’s material strategic bet or bets.
 Plain-English candidate mechanisms for each bet.
 An evaluation of all 80 strategies.
 A ranked set of every current strategy that clears the evidence, materiality, and independence thresholds.
 Zero or one unusual pair, supported by an explicit reference class, excluded alternative, interaction, and copying difficulty.
 Any “not yet” strategies that have a concrete 12-to-24-month path and a named missing condition.
 Open questions that would materially change the mapping.
 A plain-world condition that would make the overall conclusion wrong.
 “Nothing stands out” when no strategy clears the threshold.
The structured audit can retain the disposition of all 80 strategies. The eventual prose should discuss only strategies that genuinely help explain how the company expects to win.
