# How it wins strategy rubric

This rubric applies Cold Start's [judgment standard](./2026-08-21-how-it-wins-judgment-standard.md) to Steph Ango's [Many ways to win](https://stephango.com/moats). Strategy names and canonical meanings come from `HOW_IT_WINS_STRATEGIES` in `packages/core/src/how-it-wins.ts`, which follows Steph's source. The other columns are Cold Start operational tests. They are not additions to Steph's definitions.

Positive evidence makes a strategy a candidate. It does not make the strategy current unless the full judgment standard passes. Missing evidence is insufficient evidence, not disqualifying evidence.

## Accumulation

| Strategy | Steph's canonical meaning | Positive operational evidence | Common false positives | Nearest siblings | Deciding question | Disqualifying evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Usership | A critical mass of users makes the product more useful to each of them. | More active users increase utility for existing users through counterparties, matching, shared content, interoperability, or contributions. | Customer count; famous users; revenue growth; vendor learning; uptime from scale. | Aggregation; Emergence; Omnipresence; Antifragility; Reliability. | Does another user's participation increase existing-user value? | Users are isolated; utility does not rise with participation; value comes from vendor inputs alone. |
| Completeness | One tool covers everything the buyer needs, so nothing else is required. | The offering covers the defined job well enough to retire or avoid other tools. | Many features; end-to-end claims; broad use cases; a suite or marketplace. | Versatility; Bundling; Aggregation; Composability; Omnipresence. | Does it remove the need for another tool across the defined job? | Core work still needs outside tools; customers assemble a stack; the company remains a point solution. |
| Aggregation | An environment or marketplace built for broad participation. | Suppliers, creators, developers, buyers, or other parties contribute material value inside a shared venue. | Large audience; company-owned catalog; integrations; a closed or tightly curated set. | Usership; Curation; Union; Emergence; Omnipresence. | Is broad participation in a shared environment the mechanism? | The company supplies nearly all value; outside participation is closed or immaterial. |
| Diversification | Low dependency on any one stream of customers, suppliers, or money. | Independent revenue, customer, supplier, geography, product, or capital streams reduce dependence on one failure mode. | A long customer list; several products sharing one budget; nominal geographic spread; organizational diversity. | Decentralization; Versatility; Aggregation; Modularity. | Does the advantage come from reduced dependence on one economic or resource stream? | One stream sustains the whole; nominally separate streams share the same material risk. |
| Omnipresence | Available everywhere it might be needed and accepted by default. | The offering is embedded, distributed, integrated, or accepted across the important places and moments of need. | Large market; many countries; awareness; many integrations; Aggregation. | Aggregation; Bundling; Standardization; Usership; Completeness. | Is the advantage that the product is already present or accepted where the need occurs? | Important contexts remain absent; users must search or install; competing defaults occupy major channels. |
| Cloning | Expands by replicating identical copies of itself. | Growth repeats a proven whole unit, such as a store, franchise, facility, service pod, or deployment, with stable design and economics. | Copying a competitor; software autoscaling; reusable components; standardized procedures; bespoke expansion. | Predictability; Standardization; Modularity; Blitzing; Copycat. | Does expansion reproduce the same complete operating unit? | Each expansion is materially redesigned; local conditions change the unit or its economics. |

## Price

| Strategy | Steph's canonical meaning | Positive operational evidence | Common false positives | Nearest siblings | Deciding question | Disqualifying evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Affordability | Costs less than the alternatives. | Total buyer cost is lower than relevant alternatives and affects adoption, retention, access, or replacement. | Free trial; temporary subsidy; low sticker price with high integration cost; internal Efficiency. | Efficiency; Bundling; Skimming; Simplicity. | Is lower total buyer cost the reason for choice? | Price is at parity or premium; total cost is higher; temporary pricing lacks operating support. |
| Luxury | Costs deliberately more, often labor-intensive or a status marker. | Deliberate premium pricing, intensive quality or service, or exclusivity makes cost or status part of the value. | High cost from inefficiency; recognition alone; an expensive enterprise contract; quality without premium positioning. | Prestige; Craftsmanship; Scarcity; Rarity; Charm. | Is deliberate expensiveness or status part of the value? | The company competes on value or discounts; customers would prefer the same meaning at a lower price. |
| Skimming | Takes a large volume of tiny transactions or resources. | A small fee, take rate, or resource increment compounds across a large flow. | Low prices; subscriptions; advertising generally; marketplaces without tiny-per-flow economics. | Affordability; Aggregation; Parasitism; Chokepoint. | Does the company win by taking a small amount from each of many flows? | Economics depend on large margins from few transactions or fixed contracts unrelated to flow. |
| Bundling | Comes packaged free with something people already want. | A product is included free or at negligible incremental cost with an independently desired product, changing adoption or economics. | Multi-product suite; volume discount; cross-sell; integrated workflow; Completeness. | Completeness; Affordability; Omnipresence; Union. | Would adoption or bargaining power fall materially without the desired host product? | Customers mainly buy it alone; the package is immaterial; the host has no independent demand. |

## Time

| Strategy | Steph's canonical meaning | Positive operational evidence | Common false positives | Nearest siblings | Deciding question | Disqualifying evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Heritage | A legacy that spans generations. | Multigenerational legacy changes present trust, identity, status, provenance, or preference. | Old incorporation date; long founder tenure; nostalgia; Endurance; an unknown archive. | Endurance; Authenticity; Prestige; Organic. | Does inherited legacy itself create present value? | No meaningful generational continuity; the brand was reset; buyers do not know or value the legacy. |
| Craftsmanship | Spends more time, precision, and attention than competitors do. | The product receives more time, precision, or attention than alternatives, and the difference is visible in the result. | Premium branding; slow process; handmade claims; high cost; Precision alone. | Precision; Luxury; Organic; Specialization. | Is extra time, precision, or attention the source of advantage? | No comparative evidence of extra attention; time adds delay without better outcomes. |
| Organic | Grows through a natural process that cannot be accelerated. | Value depends on a natural maturation or growth process whose clock cannot be compressed without harming the result. | Bootstrapping; word of mouth; slow company growth; organic marketing language; Craftsmanship. | Heritage; Rarity; Craftsmanship; Endurance. | Does value depend on an irreducible natural process? | Capital, automation, or process compression can produce the same outcome; time is only delay. |
| Endurance | Keeps operating continuously over a long period. | Long continuous operation creates present trust, knowledge, access, installed position, or confidence. | Old founding date; durable object; short uptime record; Heritage. | Heritage; Reliability; Durability; Predictability. | Is continuous operation over time the operative advantage? | Material discontinuity breaks the claim; persistence has no present consequence. |

## Uniqueness

| Strategy | Steph's canonical meaning | Positive operational evidence | Common false positives | Nearest siblings | Deciding question | Disqualifying evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Specialization | Strong competence in a narrow niche. | Concentrated talent, product, data, or process focus on a narrow customer, problem, domain, or workflow produces a fit that broader rivals demonstrably lack: a measured result, a capability they do not have, or customers choosing on that fit. | Small market; one product; a company that does one thing (the default state of a startup, not a mechanism); stated focus with no superiority result; niche branding; accuracy; Curation. | Versatility; Precision; Craftsmanship; Curation. | Does the evidence show niche performance that broader rivals cannot match, not only that scope is narrow? | The company pursues broad competence; niche performance is not superior; the only evidence is that the company is small and does one thing; focus is accidental or temporary. |
| Versatility | General competence adapted to many tasks. | The same core product, capability, or team works across materially different tasks or settings. | Many separate products; bespoke customization; full coverage of one job; Hybrid. | Completeness; Hybrid; Malleability; Composability. | Is one competence useful across many tasks? | Breadth depends on unrelated products or teams; performance breaks outside a narrow core. |
| Hybrid | Competence in two distinct areas, or two strengths not usually found together. | Two distinct competencies or strengths are both materially present, especially where they usually trade off. | Any two labels; generalism; best-of-both-worlds claims; two separate products; one outsourced side. | Versatility; Metamorphosis; Specialization; Composability. | Are two independently demonstrated strengths present together? | One side is weak, aspirational, unrelated, or wholly outsourced; the combination is ordinary in the reference class. |
| Divergence | Creativity and difference from the norm. | A substantive departure from category convention in product, design, distribution, business model, or behavior affects outcomes. | Cosmetic branding; novelty; a different technical approach or product shape with no outcome tied to it; irreverent language; inconsistency; following a smaller trend. | Irreverence; Unpredictability; Charm; Copycat. | Is the advantage a substantive creative difference from the norm? | The operative product and model follow the dominant pattern; differences are cosmetic or immaterial. |
| Authenticity | Traceable to its source where counterfeits are common. | Provenance, authorship, identity, origin, or chain of custody is verifiable where false origin matters. | Founder story; transparency; heritage; respected branding; authentic voice. | Heritage; Transparency; Prestige; Security. | Is value created by proving genuine origin? | False provenance is not relevant; origin cannot be verified; buyers do not care about it. |
| Rarity | Naturally limited in quantity. | Supply is inherently constrained by nature, location, biology, time, a unique asset, or a nonrepeatable event. | Deliberate limits; temporary shortages; high price; low capacity; exclusive distribution. | Scarcity; Organic; Authenticity; Luxury. | Would quantity remain limited even if the producer wanted more? | Supply can expand through policy, capital, or ordinary production. |
| Scarcity | Artificially limited in quantity. | The company deliberately restricts quantity, access, membership, or release timing, and the limit affects demand, status, pricing, or control. | Stockouts; naturally rare inputs; small-company capacity; premium price; Curation. | Rarity; Luxury; Highest bidder; Curation. | Is supply or access intentionally restricted by the company? | Limits are accidental, unenforced, or removed whenever demand appears. |
| Secrecy | Hard to copy because the knowledge is protected or the parts cannot be disentangled. | Essential private or tacit knowledge is protected, or components are so entangled that the mechanism cannot be reconstructed easily. | Security controls; Privacy; public patents; complexity alone; Obscurity without protected knowledge. | Security; Privacy; Obscurity; Espionage. | Is copying hard because essential knowledge is inaccessible or cannot be disentangled? | The method is public or readily reverse-engineered; the protected knowledge is not needed for performance. |
| Irreverence | Contrarian and counter-cultural, disregarding tradition. | Repeated rejection of category traditions, taboos, or authority attracts users or enables consequential choices. | Edgy copy; humor; one campaign; generic Divergence; surprise alone. | Divergence; Charm; Unpredictability; Last-mover. | Is the company gaining from disregarding convention or authority? | Operations remain conventional; the posture vanishes under pressure; buyers require established legitimacy. |

## Offense

| Strategy | Steph's canonical meaning | Positive operational evidence | Common false positives | Nearest siblings | Deciding question | Disqualifying evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Violence | Destroys or consumes the opponent through force. | The company directly destroys, consumes, or removes opposing capacity through force rather than ordinary competition. | Strong growth; aggressive sales; price competition; routine acquisitions; forceful rhetoric. | Sabotage; Scavenging; Blitzing; Highest bidder. | Is direct destruction or consumption of opposing capacity the mechanism? | Wins come from buyer preference; opposing capacity remains intact; no direct force is used. |
| Litigation | Weakens opponents through legal burden. | Lawsuits, intellectual-property enforcement, or legal and regulatory processes impose material cost, delay, or restriction on opponents. | Routine disputes; compliance; defensive legal work; unused patents. | Deterrence; Monopoly; Sabotage; Security. | Is legal burden itself weakening opponents? | Action is incidental or purely defensive; opponents face no material burden. |
| Nettlesomeness | Causes the opponent to make mistakes. | The company's tactics cause an opponent to overreact, misprice, misallocate resources, or abandon a sound position. | Being annoying; speed; surprise without opponent error; direct damage. | Unpredictability; Lure; Sabotage; Irreverence. | Is the advantage created by the opponent's mistaken response? | No opponent mistake is evidenced or reasonably inferred; the company causes the result directly. |
| Sabotage | Creates weaknesses in the opponent's structure or defenses. | The company deliberately degrades a competitor's critical technology, complement, channel, supplier base, or defense. | Ordinary competition; hiring talent; price cuts; Litigation generally; provoking a mistake. | Violence; Nettlesomeness; Parasitism; Infiltration. | Does the company directly weaken an opponent's system or support structure? | Harm is incidental to winning customers; no critical structure is targeted. |
| Parasitism | Extracts or hijacks resources from a host. | The company relies on a host's audience, infrastructure, data, content, distribution, or economics while extracting material value from it. | Normal partnership; priced infrastructure; interoperability; mutual dependency; a consensual transaction fee. | Symbiosis; Skimming; Infiltration; Puppeteering; Scavenging. | Is the mechanism extraction from a continuing host rather than mutual dependence or control? | The host receives comparable benefit and can price the relationship; extraction is absent or immaterial. |
| Scavenging | Opportunistically takes in dying or weakened prey. | The company acquires customers, talent, assets, rights, or distribution from distressed or failing competitors on advantaged terms. | Ordinary acquisition; hiring from healthy rivals; Last-mover timing; restructuring an owned business. | Last-mover; Violence; Parasitism; Highest bidder. | Does the advantage depend on the target already being weakened? | Targets are healthy; the company caused the weakness; terms show no distress advantage. |
| Espionage | Acquires the opponent's secrets. | Credible evidence shows acquisition and strategic use of nonpublic competitor information. | Public benchmarking; lawful reverse engineering; ordinary competitive intelligence; customer discovery. | Secrecy; Infiltration; Copycat; Mimicry. | Is genuinely secret opponent information being acquired? | Information is public, consensually shared, or independently discovered. |
| Swarming | A concentrated attack from many individually weak agents. | Many individually weak or limited agents coordinate a concentrated attack that overwhelms a stronger target. | Large user base; distributed workforce; crowd intelligence; many employees in a centralized campaign. | Herding; Emergence; Blitzing; Decentralization. | Are many individually weak units jointly attacking? | Units are individually powerful, uncoordinated, defensive, or merely participatory. |
| Highest bidder | Overpays or outbids competitors to secure exclusivity. | The company deliberately pays more to secure exclusive talent, rights, supply, distribution, locations, or access. | High compensation; expensive acquisition; ordinary procurement; broad spending. | Blitzing; Scarcity; Monopoly; Violence. | Is exclusivity won specifically by outpaying competitors? | The resource remains available; price is at market; nonprice factors determine access. |
| Chokepoint | Controls a passage that competitors or prey must pass through. | The company controls a gateway, interface, resource, distribution point, or passage required for the relevant outcome. | Popularity; market share; Omnipresence; broad integration; an optional platform. | Monopoly; Omnipresence; Standardization; Union. | Must relevant participants pass through the controlled point? | Practical bypasses are common and economic; control belongs elsewhere; the passage is optional. |
| Puppeteering | Takes over a host's behavior and directs it. | Governance, incentives, software, contract, or dependency gives the company operative control over another actor's behavior. | Influence; recommendations; Alliance; Parasitism; ordinary employee management. | Parasitism; Infiltration; Centralization; Symbiosis. | Does the company direct the host's behavior? | The host retains meaningful autonomy and can ignore or replace the control without material consequence. |

## Defense

| Strategy | Steph's canonical meaning | Positive operational evidence | Common false positives | Nearest siblings | Deciding question | Disqualifying evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Deterrence | Projects a credible threat of retaliation. | A visible and credible ability and willingness to retaliate reduces attack, entry, appropriation, or adverse conduct. | Security controls; balance sheet; lawsuits already filed; vague threats; recognition. | Security; Litigation; Violence; Neutrality. | Is protection created by expected punishment after attack? | Retaliation is not credible or communicated; attacks carry no consequence. |
| Reliability | Reduces maintenance and downtime. | The product fails less, needs less maintenance, has less downtime, or recovers with less burden, and that difference affects buying or use. | Many users; company age; Predictability; certification; generic service levels; customer names. | Predictability; Endurance; Durability; Usership; Precision. | Does the mechanism reduce failure and upkeep? | Failures or upkeep are high; performance is category parity and immaterial; scale reduces reliability. |
| Predictability | A repeatable process with little deviation. | Outputs, delivery, cost, quality, or experience repeat with low variance that lets users plan. | Uptime; accuracy; standard formats; schedules; internal repetition with no user value. | Reliability; Precision; Standardization; Endurance. | Is low variance valuable even when no failure occurs? | Outcomes vary materially; bespoke judgment dominates; surprise is intended. |
| Unpredictability | Creates surprise, confusion, or variability. | Deliberate surprise, confusion, or variability changes how audiences, customers, gatekeepers, or opponents respond. | Chaotic pivots; Agility; creative design alone; inconsistent execution. | Agility; Nettlesomeness; Divergence; Irreverence. | Is surprise, confusion, or variability itself strategically useful? | Variability is accidental, harms the intended audience, or has no strategic consequence. |
| Decentralization | Redundancy and distributed competence remove single points of failure. | Distributed nodes, teams, suppliers, regions, or competencies provide real redundancy and continued operation. | Remote work; community participation; distributed ownership; Modularity; many offices. | Distributed ownership; Modularity; Reliability; Antifragility; Centralization. | Does distribution remove a material single point of failure? | A critical dependency remains singular; distributed parts share the same failure mode. |
| Security | Resists theft, confiscation, and unwanted access. | Architecture, controls, audits, or operating history show resistance to theft, confiscation, compromise, or unauthorized access. | Privacy policy; trade-secret protection; compliance badge alone; Reliability; Obscurity. | Privacy; Secrecy; Deterrence; Reliability. | Is the protected harm unauthorized taking or access? | Known control weaknesses contradict the claim; repeated preventable compromise remains unresolved. |
| Privacy | Protects against unwanted disclosure. | Data minimization, local processing, encryption, restricted disclosure, or verified practice protects people or customers from unwanted revelation. | Security generally; company Secrecy; anonymous branding; policy language without practice. | Security; Secrecy; Transparency; Obscurity. | Is the central harm unwanted disclosure of personal or customer information? | The model depends on broad disclosure or sale; practice contradicts the promise. |
| Durability | Physically strong and resistant to damage. | Physical design, materials, testing, repairability, or field life show resistance to wear, impact, injury, or degradation. | Software uptime; company longevity; Reliability; Craftsmanship; warranty alone. | Reliability; Endurance; Craftsmanship; Organic. | Is physical resistance to damage the advantage? | No relevant physical subject exists; ordinary use causes premature damage; disposability is intentional. |
| Neutrality | Displays long-term non-belligerence and offers a safe haven. | Repeated conduct, governance, and incentives show durable nonalignment or nonaggression that creates trust or safety. | Fairness claims; broad partnerships; compliance; openness; serving several sides. | Union; Transparency; Alliance; Decentralization. | Do participants trust the company because it credibly will not take sides or attack them? | Self-preferencing, partisan control, retaliation, or competition with dependent participants undermines neutrality. |
| Obscurity | Survives by remaining unknown or undetected. | Remaining unknown, closed, or difficult to detect reduces attack, imitation, regulation, or predation. Closed-source software may qualify when being unseen is the protective mechanism. | Small market share; weak marketing; private ownership; Secrecy when protected knowledge, not detection, is the mechanism. | Secrecy; Camouflage; Privacy; Infiltration. | Is survival or protection created by remaining unknown or undetected? | Visibility is essential; the activity is publicized; relevant adversaries already detect it. |
| Antifragility | Gets stronger when exposed to stress or usage. | Shocks, attacks, failures, volatility, or use systematically improve future capability, resilience, economics, or position. | Surviving stress; redundancy; postmortems; ordinary learning; user growth; Iteration. | Reliability; Decentralization; Iteration; Usership; Agility. | Does stress or usage leave the system stronger than before? | Stress only consumes buffers or causes damage; improvement does not accumulate. |

## Deception

| Strategy | Steph's canonical meaning | Positive operational evidence | Common false positives | Nearest siblings | Deciding question | Disqualifying evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Camouflage | Blends into the surrounding environment. | Resemblance to the surrounding environment prevents detection or distinction by a relevant observer. | White-label convenience; native experience; low profile; copying one actor; category convention. | Mimicry; Obscurity; Infiltration; Decoy. | Is concealment achieved by blending into the background? | Distinct visibility is intended; resemblance does not conceal; no detection problem exists. |
| Mimicry | Superficially adopts another's characteristics to mislead. | The company or product adopts traits of a specific trusted, dangerous, legitimate, or familiar type so others mistake it or treat it differently. | Compatibility; Copycat method; Camouflage; category familiarity; resemblance without altered treatment. | Camouflage; Copycat; Decoy; Infiltration. | Is the purpose to be mistaken for another entity or type? | Origin is clear; similarity is functional only; no mistaken identity or altered treatment occurs. |
| Decoy | Distracts or misleads adversaries. | A visible signal, offer, roadmap, target, or activity draws attention away from a material asset, vulnerability, or move. | Secondary product; loss leader; changed roadmap; Lure; experimentation. | Lure; Camouflage; Nettlesomeness; Mimicry. | Is attention diverted away from what matters? | The visible object is genuinely central; nothing material is concealed; diversion is accidental. |
| Lure | Sets attractive traps. | An attractive offer induces a target into a position where the company can capture, constrain, exploit, or disadvantage it. | Good marketing; free product; Low-friction; Bundling; mutual entry offer. | Decoy; Parasitism; Bundling; Low-friction. | Does the attraction function as a trap with an adverse consequence? | The exchange is transparent and mutually beneficial; no capture or dependency follows. |
| Infiltration | Gets past defenses without notice. | The company crosses a meaningful gate or defense without the relevant gatekeeper noticing, then establishes position. | Permitted bottom-up adoption; easy onboarding; integration; native design; Camouflage alone. | Camouflage; Parasitism; Puppeteering; Low-friction. | Did it cross a meaningful defense without notice? | Entry is explicit and visible; no meaningful defense exists. |

## Timing

| Strategy | Steph's canonical meaning | Positive operational evidence | Common false positives | Nearest siblings | Deciding question | Disqualifying evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| First-mover | Acts first to gain an advantage. | Early entry created a durable present asset such as users, data, rights, standards, locations, learning, or habit. | Earliest founding date; a "first" or "world's first" claim in company copy; first announcement; category naming; early entry with no present asset traced to it; current leadership without causal history. | Second-mover; Last-mover; Heritage; Usership. | Is the present advantage causally traceable to acting first? | The lead dissipated; later entrants gained equivalent assets; timing created no durable advantage. |
| Second-mover | Moves quickly to copy the first mover. | The company followed a pioneer quickly, copied validated elements, and gained from the pioneer's proof or mistakes. | Any Copycat; entering an established market; Iteration; much later entry. | Copycat; Last-mover; Iteration; First-mover. | Is the advantage specifically fast following after the first mover? | Entry was much later or independent; no learning from the pioneer is evidenced. |
| Last-mover | Waits until opponents have spent themselves on failed approaches. | The company waited while others spent resources on failed approaches, then committed after important uncertainty cleared. | Any late entrant; slow execution; Scavenging; fast following. | Second-mover; Scavenging; Curation; First-mover. | Did waiting through others' failed approaches create the advantage? | Lateness was accidental; no avoided cost or learning is evidenced; the market was already closed. |

## Accreditation

| Strategy | Steph's canonical meaning | Positive operational evidence | Common false positives | Nearest siblings | Deciding question | Disqualifying evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Monopoly | Control of a resource or market approved by a governing body. | A license, charter, concession, patent right, allocation, or other governing-body act grants exclusive control. | Market dominance; Chokepoint; de facto standard; intellectual property without authorized exclusivity. | Chokepoint; Litigation; Standardization; Highest bidder. | Does exclusivity derive from governing-body approval? | Equivalent rights are broadly available; no formal grant exists; the right expired or is nonexclusive. |
| Prestige | Endorsed by authoritative sources through awards, degrees, or recognition. | Authoritative recognition changes buyer selection, willingness to pay, or trust. | Top-tier investors; cap table; famous customers; celebrity association; media attention; founder pedigree. | Luxury; Heritage; Authenticity; Curation. | Would the buyer's decision materially change without the recognition? | Recognition is unknown or irrelevant to buyers; it affects only investors or recruiting; the authority lacks credibility. |
| Curation | Selective, with a particular ability to choose and group. | The company deliberately excludes, ranks, selects, or groups options, and users rely on that judgment. | Aggregation; recommendation without selectivity; Specialization; Prestige; a small catalog from limited supply. | Aggregation; Specialization; Prestige; Centralization. | Is value created by what is deliberately left out and how the rest is grouped? | Nearly everything is accepted; payment or popularity alone decides; users do not rely on the selection. |

## Collaboration

| Strategy | Steph's canonical meaning | Positive operational evidence | Common false positives | Nearest siblings | Deciding question | Disqualifying evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Union | Reduces friction between potential competitors through a common set of rules. | Potential competitors adopt common rules, governance, or structures that reduce friction, including combinations such as mergers or political unions. | Bilateral partnership; de facto standard alone; inactive association; unilateral platform terms. | Alliance; Standardization; Neutrality; Herding. | Are potential competitors joined through a common rule system or structure that reduces friction? | No common rules or structure exist; one actor's unilateral terms are the whole arrangement; participants are not potential competitors. |
| Alliance | A partnership with some benefits of a union while staying independent. | Independent organizations coordinate technology, distribution, resources, legitimacy, or access for material mutual benefit while preserving independence. | Vendor contract; customer logo; named customers or integrations; co-marketing; a corporate investor; a stated plan to deepen partnerships; announcement; Symbiosis; Union. | Symbiosis; Union; Highest bidder; Bundling. | Do the parties remain independent and viable outside the partnership? | Benefit is trivial or one-way; dependence breaks a party's core model; the parties no longer remain independent. |
| Emergence | The wisdom of a crowd. | Decentralized participant interaction produces useful knowledge, order, prediction, quality, or behavior no central actor supplied alone. | Large user base; Aggregation; centrally keyed crowdsourcing; popularity; content volume. | Usership; Aggregation; Swarming; Centralization; Curation. | Does collective interaction produce an outcome no participant or central planner supplied? | Output is centrally determined; the crowd only supplies volume; interaction adds no collective result. |
| Centralization | A single decision-making entity. | Concentrated decision rights create consequential coherence, speed, consistency, bargaining power, control, or accountability. | Founder-led company; centralized database; Curation; ordinary hierarchy; Monopoly. | Decentralization; Union; Curation; Puppeteering. | Is the advantage caused by one decision-making authority? | Key actors retain independent vetoes; authority is fragmented; the bottleneck adds cost without advantage. |
| Standardization | Emergent alignment that reduces friction. | Independent actors converge on a common format, protocol, interface, or practice that reduces coordination, switching, or integration friction. | Internal procedure; Predictability; formal Union rules; one vendor's proprietary format. | Union; Predictability; Modularity; Omnipresence; Chokepoint. | Has alignment emerged as a shared norm across independent actors? | Adoption is confined to one organization; incompatible variants dominate; alignment does not reduce friction. |
| Symbiosis | A mutually beneficial dependency between two organisms. | Two parties co-specialize so each receives material benefit and depends on the other's continued function. | Partnership; integration; customer-vendor relationship; Alliance; Parasitism; Bundling. | Alliance; Parasitism; Bundling; Union. | Would either party's core value or economics weaken materially if the other disappeared, and is that dependence mutual? | Either party can replace the relationship easily; benefit is one-way or incidental. |
| Herding | Many individuals grouping to protect against larger competitors. | Many smaller actors cluster or pool resources chiefly to protect themselves from a larger threat. | Aggregation; Union; user network; Swarming attack; ordinary coalition. | Swarming; Union; Aggregation; Decentralization. | Is the group's primary function collective defense against a stronger actor? | The group exists for commerce, discovery, production, offense, or social participation without a shared threat. |
| Distributed ownership | Owned by the community. | Users, workers, contributors, or community members hold meaningful economic ownership or governance rights. | Distributed users; open-source contribution; token without rights; advisory vote; Decentralization. | Decentralization; Union; Transparency; Centralization. | Do community participants own residual value or meaningful governance control? | Rights are symbolic, revocable, or noneconomic; ownership remains concentrated. |
| Transparency | An open and visible process that invites trust. | Product workings, code, pricing, governance, sourcing, decisions, or performance are inspectable in a way that creates trust or accountability. | Marketing disclosure; repository that omits the operative process; Authenticity; Privacy policy; selective metrics. | Authenticity; Privacy; Neutrality; Secrecy; Curation. | Does inspectability of how the company works or decides create trust? | Important workings remain hidden or misleading; disclosure creates a false picture; trust does not depend on visibility. |

## Speed and scale

| Strategy | Steph's canonical meaning | Positive operational evidence | Common false positives | Nearest siblings | Deciding question | Disqualifying evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Iteration | Iterates and changes quickly. | Evidence identifies the feedback, the resulting change, and a subsequent outcome or next learning cycle, at a cadence that matters in the category. | Release count; announcements; fundraising; experiments; growth; team size. | Agility; Antifragility; Second-mover; Precision. | What feedback entered, what changed because of it, and what result fed the next cycle? | Changes are unrelated to feedback; cadence is immaterial; no observation-to-change-to-outcome loop exists. |
| Efficiency | Uses fewer resources than competitors for similar capability. | Comparable capability uses less compute, labor, capital, energy, time, inventory, or another material resource under relevant conditions. | Lower buyer price; fewer features; shifted cost; small team; low burn or bootstrapping with no per-unit comparison; Simplicity; lower quality. | Affordability; Simplicity; Precision; Skimming. | Does the company use fewer total resources for comparable capability? | Savings come from lower performance or shifted cost; no valid baseline exists; advantage disappears at scale. |
| Agility | Adapts easily to a changing environment. | The company or product changes direction, configuration, resource allocation, or response with low delay and restructuring cost. | Frequent pivots; Iteration; Unpredictability; customization; emergency reaction. | Iteration; Malleability; Unpredictability; Modularity. | Can it adapt to external change easily? | Architecture, contracts, incentives, or organization make adaptation slow or require rebuilding. |
| Precision | High accuracy and exactness in performance or output. | Accuracy, tolerance, targeting, calibration, or exactness materially exceeds alternatives and improves the relevant outcome. | Reliability; Predictability; Craftsmanship; Specialization; polished output; confidence language. | Reliability; Predictability; Craftsmanship; Specialization. | Is correctness or exactness of each output the advantage? | Accuracy is unmeasured, comparable, or worse; the task does not reward exactness. |
