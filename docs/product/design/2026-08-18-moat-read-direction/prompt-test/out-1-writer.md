Irregular runs simulations in which a frontier AI model is set loose, in both attacker and defender roles, inside a full network environment with no planted vulnerabilities, then scored on tasks like antivirus evasion and system infiltration [e1][e2][e3][e7]. It sells this testing directly to the labs that build the models it is testing. Founded in 2023 as Pattern Labs, the company raised $80 million across a seed and Series A that closed within weeks of each other in September 2025, led by Sequoia Capital and Redpoint Ventures, with Wiz CEO Assaf Rappaport and Eon CEO Ofir Ehrlich as named angel investors [e1][e3][e4][e5]. Its reported customers are OpenAI and Anthropic, with government bodies including the UK government also described as clients, and the company says it was already generating millions of dollars in revenue at the time of the raise [e1][e2][e3]. Headcount is small, 37 people, with 42 percent of the org classified as research staff [e7].

**Hybrid.** The company's method combines two skill sets that are not usually housed in the same shop: offensive cybersecurity red-teaming and frontier AI capability evaluation. Most AI safety evaluators come out of the alignment research world and test models against fixed question sets or synthetic benchmarks. Irregular instead builds live network environments, with no scripted vulnerabilities inserted for the model to find, and has the model attack and defend within them [e1][e2][e3][e7]. That is a cybersecurity red-team practice applied to a model-evaluation problem, which requires people fluent in both real intrusion techniques and current model behavior. This is a specific, checkable claim about method, not a branding choice: the named benchmarks (Atomic Tasks, CyScenarioBench, FrontierCyber) are built to test whether a model can actually complete tasks like antivirus evasion on live infrastructure, not whether it can answer a written question about how antivirus evasion works. Whether the founders themselves bring deep prior expertise in either domain is not something the evidence settles. Dan Lahav's public history before Irregular consists of a small nonprofit initiative and a lecturer post at Tel Aviv University [e12]; Omer Nevo's LinkedIn shows a nonprofit board role and CEO title but no named prior technical employer [e13]. The card's own read on this notes the 22-plus years of experience attributed to Nevo cannot be independently verified beyond the summary claim [e13][e2]. So the hybrid method is observable in the product; the founders' personal command of it is inference, not confirmed fact.

**An emerging chokepoint.** The clearest evidence of something more than vendor status is that Irregular's scoring framework and benchmark names are reportedly cited in OpenAI's system cards for o3 and o4-mini, and used by Anthropic to assess cyber risk in Claude 3.7 Sonnet [e1][e2][e3][s3]. If accurate, this means two competing labs are referencing the same external framework, by name, in the formal documentation that accompanies a model release. That is a different position than being one option among several red-team vendors: once a system card cites a specific benchmark as the basis for a cyber-risk claim, switching evaluators for the next model generation creates a visible break in the record that safety researchers, journalists, or regulators could flag as either backsliding or standard-shopping. This gives Irregular's specific methodology some stickiness across model generations, at least for these two customers. I want to be precise about what this evidence does and does not show. It shows adoption by two labs, not an industry-wide requirement. It does not show that OpenAI or Anthropic could not switch evaluators at acceptable cost, only that doing so would carry a cost the evidence lets us estimate but not measure. So "chokepoint" here describes a mechanism in formation, built on two data points, not an established structural lock.

**Prestige.** The company is validated less by its own claims than by who is willing to be named alongside it. Its funding round was led by Sequoia and Redpoint, with personal investment from the CEO of Wiz and the CEO of Eon, both cybersecurity company builders whose personal money is a specific, harder-to-fake signal than a general VC check [e1][e2][e3]. Its customer list, independently reported by TechCrunch and Calcalist rather than only claimed on Irregular's own site, includes OpenAI and Anthropic by name, which is unusual for a 2023-founded, 37-person firm [e1][e2][p1]. These labs are known to be selective about which outside parties get access to pre-release models, so a confirmed contract functions as third-party endorsement even without disclosed contract terms.

The pairing worth flagging as unusual for this category is Hybrid and the emerging chokepoint together, not either alone. Plenty of boutique AI red-team shops exist and are narrowly specialized; plenty of cybersecurity vendors have built durable businesses around being cited in a customer's compliance documentation. What is harder to find is a narrow specialist whose specific benchmark names get referenced, independently, inside the formal release documentation of two rival labs at the same time. That combination is hard to copy for a structural reason: a competitor cannot win this position simply by building a better red-team benchmark. It would need both OpenAI and Anthropic to adopt its framework in place of one that is already sitting in their prior system cards, and switching creates a visible discontinuity in each lab's own safety record that neither company has an obvious incentive to create just to change vendors. The barrier is not proprietary technology; it is the cost, to the customer, of unwinding a citation that already exists in a public document.

There are two ways Irregular could extend its position that the evidence does not yet show it has taken. One is Standardization: its own press release is titled "set the security standards for frontier AI" [e3], but the evidence only supports adoption by two labs so far, not an industry-wide protocol that other AI labs, auditors, or evaluators independently converge on. That would require Google DeepMind, Meta, or a standards body to adopt the same framework as a baseline, which the current evidence does not confirm; the company's own description lists these as parties whose models it tests, without confirming they are paying customers on the same footing as OpenAI and Anthropic. The second is Monopoly: Irregular already works with government bodies including the UK government [e2], but nothing in the evidence shows a regulator has formally designated it as a required or approved evaluator rather than one vendor a government body chose to hire. That would need an explicit accreditation or legal mandate naming Irregular or its methodology, which has not happened based on what is here.

A few things would change this read. If it turns out OpenAI or Anthropic could swap in an alternative evaluator at low cost and without any visible break in their safety documentation, the chokepoint claim weakens to ordinary vendor status. If a third-party red-team firm gets its own benchmarks cited in a future system card from either lab, the exclusivity of the current pairing erodes. If revenue disclosure ever shows that one customer accounts for most of Irregular's revenue, the picture of diversified demand implied by a four-lab, one-government client list would need to be corrected toward dependency risk instead. And since neither founder's prior technical track record is independently documented beyond LinkedIn titles and durations, any future evidence about their actual background, positive or negative, would change how much of this company's position should be credited to founder expertise versus to being early into a category that barely existed before 2023.
